The anterior perineal triangle is layered much like the abdominal wall — skin, subcutaneous tissue, then a fascial sheet — and its fibrous attachments decide where blood and infection can travel.

Two triangles

A shared base between the tuberosities

Anterior and posterior perineal triangles meet along a line between the ischial tuberosities. Outer borders are the bony outlet: pubic arch and ischiopubic rami in front, sacrotuberous ligament and coccyx behind.

Roof

One superior boundary for both

The inferior fascia of the levator ani muscles forms the superior boundary of both the anterior and the posterior triangle.

Layered plan

Familiar from the abdomen

Skin and subcutaneous tissue overlying a fascial layer — here the perineal membrane, which separates the superficial from the deep perineal pouch.

Naming

Old terms retired

Perineal membrane replaces urogenital diaphragm; membranous layer replaces Colles fascia; bulbospongiosus replaces bulbocavernosus.

Principle

The subcutaneous septa attach laterally to the ischiopubic rami and fuse posteriorly with the perineal membrane, but there is no anterior attachment to the pubic rami — so a hematoma or infection deep to the membranous layer is contained posterolaterally but free to track into the abdominal wall.

Subcutaneous emphysema at laparoscopy

Because the abdominal and perineal subcutaneous layers communicate anteriorly, carbon dioxide that extravasates into the subcutaneous layer — with accidental trocar displacement or during lengthy procedures — can produce emphysema extending from the abdominal wall down into the perineum. The same continuity explains spread of hematoma or infection in either direction.

The vulva lies on the pubic bones and extends posteriorly under the pubic arch, and each of its named parts carries its own set of lesions.

Pearl

Because the labia minora and vestibule sit on loose connective tissue rather than on fat, their skin lifts readily off the underlying tissue — the property that makes skinning vulvectomy feasible in that region.

Variation, symptoms and labial reduction

Shape and size of the labia minora vary greatly. One or both may extend well beyond the labia majora and be drawn into the vagina during coitus or other activity. Where that is associated with dyspareunia or pain, surgical reduction may be considered. Complications include hypoesthesia and paresthesias. Procedures that remove the prepuce or adjacent skin and underlying connective tissue risk injuring the dorsal nerve of the clitoris.

The perineal membrane is the reference plane: everything caudal to it is the superficial pouch, everything just deep to it is the deep pouch.

Superficial pouch

Between investing fascia and membrane

Contains the clitoris and its crura, the vestibular bulbs, and the ischiocavernosus and bulbospongiosus muscles. The erectile bodies lie on the caudal surface of the perineal membrane.

Deep pouch

Just deep to the membrane

Holds the compressor urethrae and sphincter urethrovaginalis — the striated urogenital sphincter muscles that arch over the urethra.

Perineal fascia

One covering, three muscles

Bulbospongiosus, ischiocavernosus and superficial transverse perineal are all covered by perineal fascia, which is continuous with the clitoral fascia.

Posterior triangle

Ischioanal fossa

Bounded superomedially by levator ani and laterally by obturator internus, with the pudendal canal and neurovascular bundle on its lateral wall.

Muscles of the superficial pouch

  • Ischiocavernosus — from the ischial tuberosities and the free surfaces of the crura, inserting on the upper crura and often on the clitoral body.
  • Bulbospongiosus — originates in the perineal body and partially covers the vestibular bulbs.
  • Superficial transverse perineal — a few fibres arising with ischiocavernosus from the ischial tuberosity, running transversely toward the lateral margins of the perineal body.

Ischiocavernosus and bulbospongiosus both insert into the body of the clitoris and act to pull it downward.

Pearl

The perineal membrane is not a two-layered structure with muscle between, which is why the term urogenital diaphragm was abandoned. It lies just caudal to the skeletal muscle of the striated urogenital sphincter.

The clitoris is a midline body topped by the glans with paired crura, and unlike the penis it has no functional relationship to the urethra.

Origin

Embryologically derived from the genital tubercle; homologous with the penis.

Crura

Bend downward from the body, firmly attached to the pubic bones, continuing dorsally on the inferior aspects of the ischiopubic rami before joining in the midline.

Body

Paired corpora cavernosa separated in the midline by the septum of the corpora cavernosa; corpora and crura are invested by the tunica albuginea.

Suspension

The body is suspended from the pubic bones by the subcutaneous suspensory and fundiform ligaments. The fundiform ligament descends from the linea alba above the symphysis, splits around the body, then fuses with the clitoral fascia.

Vestibular bulbs

Paired 3–4 cm masses of richly vascular spongy erectile tissue immediately under the vestibular skin, joining anteriorly at the commissure of the bulbs where the tissue attaches to the undersurface of the glans and body.

Autonomic innervation

  • Erectile tissue is supplied by the cavernous nerves of the clitoris, distal extensions of the uterovaginal plexus — itself a component of the inferior hypogastric plexus.
  • These fibres run in paravaginal and paraurethral connective tissue and reach the perineum by passing under the pubic bones, joining the dorsal nerve of the clitoris to supply the corpora cavernosa.
  • They are of very small calibre and can only be confirmed microscopically. They carry both sympathetic and parasympathetic components and are critical to sexual function.
Watch for

Injury to the inferior hypogastric plexus during radical hysterectomy or other extensive pelvic or perineal surgery can cause varying degrees of voiding, sexual and defecatory dysfunction. Anti-incontinence procedures that pass sutures or trocars through paraurethral tissue may disrupt the same fibres within the retropubic space.

Where the dorsal nerve sits relative to the fascial layers

The dorsal nerve and vessels of the clitoris lie outside the tunica albuginea but within the clitoral fascia, which is continuous with the deep part of the suspensory ligament. Along the dorsal surface of the body the nerve remains deep to the clitoral fascia and superficial to the tunica albuginea, giving small branches to the prepuce skin and to the corpora cavernosa before perforating the glans.

The pudendal nerve is the main sensory and motor nerve of the perineum, and its vessels run the same course throughout.

Step walkthrough

Course of the pudendal nerve and vessels

Step 1 of 3 · Territory

What it has to reach

The nerve arises from the sacral plexus (S2–S4); the accompanying arteries arise from the anterior division of the internal iliac artery.

  • Sensation to the external genitalia.
  • Motor supply to the superficial perineal muscles, parts of the striated urethral sphincter, and the external anal sphincter.
Step 2 of 3 · Exit and re-entry

Around the ischial spine, into Alcock canal

  • Leaves the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen, passing behind the sacrospinous ligament just medial to the ischial spine.
  • Enters the pudendal (Alcock) canal through the lesser sciatic foramen.
  • The canal is formed by a splitting of the obturator fascia over the medial surface of obturator internus, running roughly from the ischial spine to the ischial tuberosity.
Step 3 of 3 · Terminal branches

Three branches, three territories

The dorsal nerve of the clitoris stays in the deep pouch adherent to the inner surface of the ischiopubic ramus, perforates the perineal membrane, then runs on the deep surface of ischiocavernosus and the crus.

  • About 2–3 cm lateral to the mid symphysis it emerges from the deep lateral surface of the crus.
  • Consistently 2–4 mm in diameter in that region.
  • Runs on the dorsal clitoral body at roughly the 11 and 1 o’clock positions.

Anatomical variant

The inferior rectal nerve does not enter the pudendal canal in about 50% of cadaveric specimens examined. That matters in procedures entering the ischioanal fossa and in radiographically guided injections used for pain management.

Contributions to the striated urogenital sphincter

Data are limited, but branches of the perineal nerve may supply the distal part of the striated urogenital sphincter muscles — the compressor urethrae and urethrovaginalis — which lie in the deep perineal pouch superior to the perineal membrane. The dorsal nerve of the clitoris may also contribute branches to these structures.

Vulvar lymphatic drainage runs anteriorly and laterally to the superficial inguinal nodes, and the pattern sets the lateral border of resection for vulvar malignancy.

Superficial inguinal nodes

  • 12–20 nodes in a T-shaped distribution, parallel to and about 1 cm below the inguinal ligament, with the stem running down along the saphenous vein.
  • Often divided into four quadrants centred on the saphenous opening (fossa ovalis).
  • Vulvar drainage goes primarily to the medial nodes of the upper quadrant.
  • They lie deep in the adipose layer of the subcutaneous tissues, in the membranous layer, just superficial to the fascia lata.

Deep inguinal nodes

  • One to three nodes in the femoral canal of the femoral triangle.
  • Reached by lymphatics passing through the saphenous opening in the fascia lata, about 3 cm below the inguinal ligament, lateral to the pubic tubercle.
  • The membranous layer spans that opening as a trabeculated sheet, the cribriform fascia, pierced by lymphatics.
Principle

The lymphatic channels draining tissues outside the hymenal ring lie medial to the labiocrural fold, which establishes that fold as the lateral border of surgical resection for vulvar malignancies.

Pearl

Injection studies show urethral lymphatic drainage terminates in either the right or the left inguinal nodes. The clitoris has been said to drain directly to deep pelvic nodes, bypassing the superficial group, but the clinical significance of this appears minimal.

Superficial vessels crossing the field at groin dissection

Within 2 cm of the inguinal ligament several superficial vessels branch from the saphenous vein and femoral artery as the great saphenous vein joins the femoral vein through the saphenous opening:

  • Superficial epigastric — to the subcutaneous tissues of the lower abdomen.
  • Superficial circumflex iliac — laterally toward the iliac crest.
  • Superficial external pudendal — to the mons, labia majora and prepuce of the clitoris.
Medial thigh compartment — muscles, supply and deficits

One of three anatomic divisions of the thigh; its muscles principally adduct the thigh at the hip.

  • Pectineus — pectineal line of the pubis to the femur; contributes to the floor of the femoral triangle; primary action hip flexion. Transitional between anterior and medial compartments, with dual innervation but primary supply from the femoral nerve.
  • Adductor longus — superior pubic ramus to femur; forms the medial border of the femoral triangle; adducts and flexes.
  • Gracilis — body of pubis and upper half of the inferior ramus to the proximal medial tibia; the most superficial muscle of the compartment; crosses hip and knee.
  • Adductor brevis — beneath adductor longus, between the anterior and posterior branches of the obturator nerve.
  • Adductor magnus — largest and most posterior; adductor part from the ischiopubic ramus, hamstring head from the ischial tuberosity to the medial femoral epicondyle, the latter supplied by the tibial branch of the sciatic nerve.
  • Obturator externus — deep to the adductors; laterally rotates the hip, as obturator internus does.

Blood supply comes from the femoral and obturator arteries; the profunda femoris supplies pectineus and the three adductors, and its medial circumflex femoral branch perforates adductor brevis, obturator externus and gracilis. The obturator artery divides into anterior and posterior branches encircling the obturator membrane. The obturator nerve divides similarly on the anterior and posterior aspects of adductor brevis.
