The abdominal wall is a stack of layers with predictable fibre directions, and almost every surgical decision about it — incision direction, closure tension, trocar placement — follows from knowing which layer you are in and which way its fibres run.

Boundaries

Where the wall begins and ends

Above, the lower rib margin; below, the iliac crests, inguinal ligaments and pubic bones; posterolaterally it ends at the lumbar spine and its muscles.

Fibre direction

Transverse dominance

Dermal fibres run mostly transversely along a gently upward-curving line, so a vertical incision is held under more tension and heals to a wider scar.

Two muscle groups

Midline and flank

Vertical midline muscles (rectus abdominis, pyramidalis) and three flat flank muscles whose aponeuroses fuse across the midline to build the rectus sheath.

Entry point

The umbilicus

All layers fuse here, which makes it the usual endoscopic entry — and puts it directly over the aortic bifurcation and the iliac vein confluence.

Principle

Because the flank muscles pull transversely, a vertical suture line in the rectus sheath sits under more tension than a transverse one — which is why vertical incisions are more prone to dehiscence.

Why the fatty and membranous layers are regions, not sheets

Neither is a discrete, well-defined layer. They describe regions within one continuous subcutaneous tissue in which the ratio of fat to fibrous septa shifts — fat-dominant superficially, fibrous-dominant close to the rectus sheath. The membranous layer is not seen as a defined layer in a midline vertical incision at all; it is most evident at the lateral borders of a low transverse incision, just above the sheath.

Working from skin to peritoneum, each layer has a characteristic feel, a characteristic attachment, and a characteristic way of going wrong.

Step walkthrough

Crossing the lower abdominal wall, layer by layer

Step 1 of 5 · Flank muscles

External oblique first

The most superficial flat muscle. Fibres run obliquely downward and forward from the lower eight ribs to the iliac crest, pubic tubercle and linea alba.

  • Its thickened lower free edge forms the inguinal ligament.
  • It becomes aponeurotic around the midclavicular line — and more laterally as you move down.
Step 2 of 5 · Deeper flank layers

Internal oblique and transversus abdominis

Internal oblique fibres fan upward and medially and are generally perpendicular to the external oblique — but in the lower abdomen they arch caudally and run in a similar direction to it.

  • The caudal transversus fuses with internal oblique as the conjoint tendon (inguinal falx).
  • That fusion is why only two layers are discernible at the lateral end of a low transverse incision.
Step 3 of 5 · Rectus sheath

The arcuate line changes everything

Above the arcuate line the sheath splits to lie both in front of and behind the rectus. In the lower quarter it lies entirely anterior, and the rectus muscle sits directly on transversalis fascia.

  • Transition point: about one-third of the way from umbilicus to pubic crest.
  • A vertical incision reaching the umbilicus therefore has to divide the posterior sheath.
Step 4 of 5 · Peritoneum

Five folds converge on the umbilicus

  • One median umbilical fold over the median umbilical ligament (urachal remnant).
  • Paired medial umbilical folds over the obliterated umbilical arteries.
  • Paired lateral umbilical folds over the inferior epigastric vessels — transecting these bleeds significantly.

The bladder reflection is triangular with its apex highest in the midline, so opening the peritoneum lateral to the midline is less likely to injure the bladder.

Step 5 of 5 · Lateral landmark

Round ligament and the deep ring

Trace the round ligament to where it enters the deep inguinal ring: the inferior epigastric vessels lie just medial to that point.

  • The round ligament passes lateral to the deep inferior epigastric vessels, through the canal, to the labium majus.
  • The ilioinguinal nerve and the genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve also run in the canal.
Rectus abdominis

Three to four tendinous inscriptions

Sternum and costal cartilages 5–7 to the anterior pubic bone. At each inscription the sheath is firmly attached to muscle, so the two are hard to separate.

Pyramidalis

Highly variable, firmly midline

From the pubic bones anterior to rectus into the linea alba a few centimetres above the symphysis. Its midline grip makes blunt separation here difficult.

Transversalis fascia

The extraperitoneal gateway

Visible suprapubically just under the rectus muscles; incised or bluntly swept off the bladder to take tissues down by layers into the retropubic space.

Umbilical ring

A defect in the linea alba

The window the fetal umbilical vessels used, and the route umbilical hernias take. The umbilical fascia behind it is a thickening of transversalis fascia.

Pearl

The membranous layer is best developed laterally. Expect to see it at the lateral borders of a low transverse incision just above the rectus sheath, and not at all in a midline vertical incision.

The umbilicus as a landmark — what sits behind it

The umbilicus usually lies at the junction of the third and fourth lumbar vertebrae. That is also the level at which the iliac veins join to form the vena cava and at which the aorta bifurcates. Its skin is supplied by T10. The round ligament of the liver and the median and medial umbilical ligaments attach to the ring in inconsistent arrangements.

The rectus sheath is not one structure but two arrangements, divided by the arcuate line.

Above the arcuate line

The sheath splits around the rectus. Anterior layer from external oblique plus the split aponeurosis of internal oblique; posterior layer from transversus abdominis plus the other half of the internal oblique aponeurosis.

At the arcuate line

Roughly one-third of the way from umbilicus to pubic crest, which lies medial to the pubic tubercle.

Below the arcuate line

All aponeuroses pass in front. There is no posterior sheath; the rectus muscle lies in direct contact with transversalis fascia.

In the midline

Above the line the linea alba unites anterior and posterior layers, so sharp dissection is usually needed to separate them during a Pfannenstiel incision.

Pearl

The linea semilunaris is not simply where the three flank layers fuse — what happens at that line differs above and below the arcuate line, and a hernia through it is a Spigelian (lateral-ventral) hernia.

The flank muscles in detail

External oblique

Down and forward

Ribs 5–12 to iliac crest, pubic tubercle and linea alba. Free posterior edge of its aponeurosis is the inguinal ligament.

Internal oblique

Up and medial

Anterior two-thirds of iliac crest, lateral inguinal ligament and thoracolumbar fascia to ribs 10–12, pecten pubis and linea alba.

Transversus abdominis

Transverse throughout

Costal cartilages 7–12, thoracolumbar fascia, iliac crest and lateral third of the inguinal ligament to pubic crest, pecten pubis and linea alba.

Conjoint tendon

Behind the superficial ring

Fused caudal transversus and internal oblique. With transversalis fascia it forms the posterior wall of the inguinal canal; weakening gives a direct inguinal hernia.

Watch for

The linea semilunaris runs from the ninth costal cartilage to the pubic tubercle and marks the lateral border of the rectus. Incisions along that border risk denervating the muscle.

The inguinal canal — boundaries and contents

The canal lies at the lower edge of the musculofascial layer, superior and parallel to the inguinal ligament. The midinguinal point is halfway between the symphysis and the anterosuperior iliac spine, and the femoral pulse is palpable there. It has superficial and deep rings.

  • Roof: inferior free edge of transversus abdominis and internal oblique fibres.
  • Posterior wall: conjoint tendon with transversalis fascia.
  • Contents in the woman: round ligament to the labium majus, ilioinguinal nerve, genital branch of genitofemoral nerve.
  • Failure of the processus vaginalis to regress allows an indirect hernia through the deep ring, lateral to the inferior epigastric vessels.

Superficial and deep vessels run parallel courses toward the umbilicus, which is exactly why a single line drawn from the femoral pulse to the umbilicus predicts both.

Vessels

Skin and subcutaneous tissue

  • Superficial epigastric — diagonal, from the femoral vessels toward the umbilicus, branching widely as it nears it. Sits midway between skin and musculofascial layer, on the line from femoral pulse to umbilicus.
  • External pudendal — runs medially to the mons pubis, with many midline branches; bleeding in its territory is heavier than elsewhere in the abdominal subcutaneous tissue.
  • Superficial circumflex iliac — laterally from the femoral vessels toward the flank.

Musculofascial layer

  • Inferior epigastric (from external iliac) with two veins — originates lateral to the rectus, runs diagonally toward the umbilicus, crossing the muscle's lateral border midway between pubis and umbilicus, then lies on the muscle's dorsal surface between it and the posterior sheath, branching so there is no longer a single trunk.
  • Deep circumflex iliac — lies between internal oblique and transversus abdominis.
  • Around the umbilicus the inferior epigastric anastomoses with the superior epigastric, a branch of the internal thoracic artery.
Principle

The epigastric vessels lie about 5.5 cm from the midline just above the symphysis and about 4.5 cm from the midline at the umbilicus. Placing a trocar either lateral or medial to the line joining those two points minimises vascular injury.

Nerves

  • Supply comes from the abdominal continuation of intercostal nerves 7–11, the subcostal nerve (T12), and the iliohypogastric and ilioinguinal nerves (both L1).
  • Each intercostal nerve gives a lateral cutaneous branch, pierces the lateral border of the rectus sheath, sends a branch into the rectus, then perforates the sheath as anterior cutaneous branches to skin.
  • Between internal oblique and transversus lies a neurovascular plane equivalent to the intercostal plane, carrying the nerves and arteries of the anterolateral wall.
Hesselbach triangle and direct hernias

The angle between the inferior epigastric vessels and the lateral border of the rectus forms the apex of the inguinal (Hesselbach) triangle, whose base is the inguinal ligament. Direct inguinal hernias protrude through this area, medial to the inferior epigastric vessels — the distinction from the indirect hernia, which passes through the deep ring lateral to those vessels.

Seeing the vessels intraoperatively

The inferior epigastric vessels can often be seen directly through the peritoneum at laparoscopy. In thin patients the superficial epigastric vessels can frequently be identified by transillumination. Where neither is visible, the round ligament traced to the deep inguinal ring gives the landmark: the vessels lie just medial to it.

Six nerves account for most of the neurologic morbidity of gynecologic surgery, and each has a single characteristic position where it is vulnerable.

Positioning hazard

Three branches — femoral, lateral cutaneous nerve of the thigh, and the femoral branch of the genitofemoral nerve — pass under the inguinal ligament and can be compressed in lithotomy. In vaginal surgery, femoral nerve injury is most often attributed to positioning rather than dissection.

Retractor-related injuries in one place
  • Genitofemoral — direct pressure where it lies on psoas.
  • Lateral cutaneous nerve of thigh — a blade placed lateral to psoas.
  • Femoral — compression by the lateral blades of a self-retaining retractor.

All three share the same remedy: check blade position against the psoas muscle rather than against the wound edge.

Most abdominal wall complications are anatomical rather than technical — they follow from an incision or a suture crossing a line it should have stopped short of.

Principle

Low transverse fascial incisions should not extend beyond the lateral margins of the rectus muscles, and suture purchases should not extend lateral to the incision edges — both cross the neurovascular plane and risk nerve entrapment and inferior epigastric injury.

Entrapment

Iliohypogastric and ilioinguinal

Caught by a lateral suture purchase or cut at closure or accessory trocar insertion. Pain may declare itself months to years later.

Denervation

Incisions at the rectus border

An incision along the lateral border of the rectus denervates the muscle, which can render it atrophic and weaken the whole wall.

Cutaneous anaesthesia

The Pfannenstiel perforating nerve

Elevating the sheath off the muscle stretches the perforating nerve; it is sometimes ligated or cauterised for haemostasis, leaving an anaesthetic patch of skin.

Bleeding

Lateral umbilical folds

These cover the inferior epigastric vessels. Transecting them can cause significant bleeding.

How to avoid it
  • Place lateral trocars superior to the anterosuperior iliac spines.
  • Keep low transverse fascial incisions within the lateral borders of the rectus muscles.
  • Identify and spare nerves within the neurovascular plane where it is possible to do so.
  • Open the peritoneum lateral to the midline, where the bladder reflection sits lower.
Why a vertical incision behaves differently

Two separate facts compound. At skin level, transversely oriented dermal fibres put a vertical incision under more tension and produce a wider scar. At fascial level, the transverse orientation and transverse pull of the flank muscles place vertical suture lines in the rectus sheath under more tension than transverse ones, making vertical incisions more prone to dehiscence. A vertical incision reaching or passing the umbilicus also obliges you to incise the posterior sheath.
