The tissue connecting the pelvic organs to the pelvic wall behaves like a mesentery: it carries vessels and nerves, and it happens also to support. The spaces between those attachments are what you actually dissect.

What it is

Vessels, nerves, meshwork

Blood vessels and nerves interspersed with a supportive meshwork of irregular connective tissue containing collagen and elastin, connecting visceral muscularis to pelvic wall muscles.

What it is not

Not the abdominal ‘fascia’

It is not a layer like the rectus sheath encountered at laparotomy, and the term endopelvic fascia itself remains controversial.

Where it is thickest

Regional smooth muscle

In some areas there is considerable smooth muscle within it — most obviously in the region of the uterosacral ligaments.

Honest caveat

Separable, not separate

These layers can be separated from the viscera, much as superficial bowel wall can be artificially separated from deeper layers — but they are not themselves separate structures.

Principle

The word ligament is doing three different jobs in the pelvis: a dense band linking two bones, a ridge in the peritoneum, and a thickening of the endopelvic fascia. The genital tract ligaments are mostly the last two.

Condensations of visceral fascia on the lateral walls of the organs transmit their neurovascular supply and, in doing so, separate one surgical cleavage plane from the next.

Step walkthrough

From cleavage plane to cul-de-sac

Step 1 of 3 · The planes

Spaces are filled, not empty

Pelvic organs expand largely independently of one another because their attachments are loose, so they separate easily along lines of cleavage.

  • These cleavage planes are called spaces, but they contain fatty or areolar connective tissue.
  • They are separated from one another by the connections of the viscera to each other and to the pelvic walls.
Step 2 of 3 · Cul-de-sacs

Front and back behave differently

Properly the vesicouterine and rectouterine pouches.

  • Anteriorly the peritoneum lies several centimetres above the vagina; it is loosely applied, forming the vesicouterine fold that lifts to create a bladder flap.
  • Posteriorly the peritoneum covers the vagina, extending about 4 cm along the posterior wall below the fornix.
  • That posterior arrangement allows direct peritoneal entry from the vagina at vaginal hysterectomy, culdocentesis or colpotomy.
Step 3 of 3 · Suspension

The mesenteries of the genital tract

At the base of the broad ligament, beginning just caudal to the uterine arteries, a thickening of endopelvic fascia attaches cervix and upper vagina to the pelvic side walls.

  • Comprising the cardinal and uterosacral ligaments — the parametrium and paracervix.
  • They are mesenteries transmitting vessels and nerves from the pelvic walls to the genital tract, not bone-to-bone bands.
Pearl

The endopelvic fascia has a supportive function as well as a conduit function — it is analogous to a mesentery connecting bowel to body wall. Understanding its disposition matters equally in vaginal and abdominal surgery.

One continuous sheet of tissue carries four different names depending on which part you are looking at and what it attaches to.

Principle

The course of the ureter, as it forms a tunnel between the cardinal and uterosacral ligaments, is the point of division between those two structures.

Composition differs between the two

Uterosacral

Predominantly smooth muscle

With the autonomic nerves of the pelvic organs and some intermixed connective tissue and blood vessels.

Cardinal

Predominantly perivascular

Perivascular connective tissue, nerves and pelvic vessels surrounding the uterine and vaginal arteries and veins.

Uterosacral attachment

Discrete, then fanned

Discrete near the cervix, fanning out in the retroperitoneal layer to a broad, ill-defined attachment over the second, third and fourth sacral segments.

Cardinal attachment

Onto piriformis

Attaches to the cervix below the isthmus and fans out onto the pelvic walls over the piriformis muscle in the region of the greater sciatic foramen.

Orientation pearl

The cardinal ligaments are often drawn extending laterally from the cervix, but in the standing position they are almost vertical — as you would expect of suspensory tissue.

Surgical boundary

At radical pelvic surgery, the cardinal ligaments form the boundary between the anterior paravesical space and the posterior pararectal space. The uterosacral ligaments hold the cervix posteriorly over the levator plate.

Failure of the vagina’s attachments, together with levator ani damage, produces prolapse — and which segment fails determines which repair is appropriate.

Anterior compartment

  • Comprises the anterior vaginal wall and its endopelvic fascia attachments to the pelvic sidewall at the tendinous arch of the pelvic fascia.
  • Between vagina and bladder lies the vesicovaginal space; between cervix and bladder the vesicocervical space.
  • These two are separated only by the attachment of the anterior vaginal wall to the cervix and by augmented bands attaching the lower pole of the bladder to the anterior cervix — often called the supravaginal septum.
Principle

At anterior colpotomy for vaginal hysterectomy, the median dissection distance from the initial incision at the cervicovaginal junction to the anterior peritoneal reflection is about 3.4 cm.

Posterior compartment

Upper third

Supported by the uterosacral ligaments and bounded posteriorly by the cul-de-sac.

Middle

The rectovaginal space begins distal to the cul-de-sac peritoneum and extends down to the perineal body. Histologically a loose fibroadipose layer with slightly interspersed bands of fibrous tissue.

Distal third

Separated from the anal wall by the perineal body, which includes the anal sphincter muscles. No histological plane of separation exists here between vaginal wall and anus.

Failure patterns

Rectocele, enterocele, perineal bulge, or a combination — partly explained by defect location and by changes in the associated muscle, connective tissue and nerves.

Repairs

Identifying loss of support at distal, mid or proximal segments directs the choice between perineorrhaphy, posterior colporrhaphy and sacral colpoperineopexy.

The rectovaginal fascia question

Descriptions of the tissue between vagina and rectum vary, but there is growing consensus that there is no true rectovaginal fascia or Denonvilliers fascia. Despite the histological findings, the Terminologia Anatomica still includes the term.

What is actually plicated at posterior colporrhaphy

The rectovaginal space can generally be developed without difficulty below the posterior peritoneal reflection for 4–5 cm to the level of the perineal body apex. No discrete separate fascial layer has been demonstrated, but lateral projections of vaginal adventitia to the endopelvic fascia, pelvic sidewall connective tissue and levator ani muscles have been consistently observed.

The tissue plicated at posterior colporrhaphy is therefore likely derived from a splitting of the posterior vaginal wall — muscularis and adventitia — and/or the anterior rectal wall. Gross examination misleads: handling the tissue can artificially create a layer that is then misconstrued as a separate fascia.

Five named planes account for most pelvic dissection, and each is defined by the attachments that bound it rather than by anything it contains.

Pearl

Keeping the anterior–posterior asymmetry in mind — peritoneum well above the vagina in front, directly on the vagina behind — is what facilitates entering both cul-de-sacs at vaginal hysterectomy.

Bleeding in the retropubic space

The vesical venous plexus lies lateral to the bladder and vesical neck, and the dorsal veins of the clitoris drain into it. These veins bleed when sutures are placed in paravaginal and paravesical tissue, but the ooze usually stops when the sutures are tied. Dissection over the superior pubic rami should be careful, because accessory obturator connections are commonly present there.

Urethral support is real but secondary: the primary determinant of stress incontinence is the strength of the sphincter mechanism.

Principle

It was previously thought that urethral support was the primary factor determining stress incontinence. More recent work shows the strength of the urethral sphincter mechanism — low urethral closure pressure — is primary, with support playing a secondary role.

How the support layer works

Distal urethra

Inseparable from the vagina because of their shared derivation from the urogenital sinus. Fixed in position by connections of the periurethral tissues and vagina to the pubic bones through the perineal membrane.

From the midurethra upward

A hammock-like layer of endopelvic fascia and anterior vaginal wall supports the proximal urethra.

Lateral stabilisation

By attachments to both the tendinous arch of the pelvic fascia and the medial margin of the levator ani muscles.

Dynamic element

Because the endopelvic fascia attaches to muscle, levator contraction and relaxation elevate the urethra and let it descend.

On straining

Raised abdominal pressure on the ventral surface of the urethra compresses it closed against that layer. An unyielding layer is a firm backstop; an unstable one compromises closure.

Why the attachments matter more than the tissue

The stability of the supportive layer — not its bulk — determines the effectiveness of closure. The integrity of the attachment to the tendinous arch of the fascia and the levator ani is therefore critical to the stress continence mechanism.

Where this connects to the rest of the chapter

The same tendinous arch appears in the anterior vaginal compartment as the sidewall attachment of the anterior vaginal wall, and in the retropubic space as the dorsolateral limit of that space. The levator ani whose medial margin stabilises the hammock is the pubovisceral muscle described in Module 3, and the perineal membrane fixing the distal urethra is the sheet covered there as well.
