The design problem

Close the outlet, pass the fetus

The floor must resist downward force yet allow passage of a large, cranially dominant human fetus — so it is a shelf with a cleft, not a sheet.

Two systems

Muscle plus fibrous tethers

A muscular diaphragm with covering fasciae, and a series of visceral ligaments and fasciae holding the organs over the closed parts of that diaphragm.

Weak points

Where the openings are

Ancillary fibrous elements are concentrated over the openings in the pelvic diaphragm and perineal membrane, supporting the viscera exactly where the muscle cannot.

Examination

Standing, not lithotomy

Lithotomy relaxes the musculature. The closure mechanism is best appreciated in the standing patient — and in the nullipara, where a speculum is hard to insert against contracted muscle.

Principle

Textbook depictions of the levator ani are distorted by the extreme abdominal pressures of embalming, which force the muscles downward. Many illustrations therefore fail to show how horizontal this supportive shelf actually is.

Ischioanal fossa — boundaries and contents

In the posterior triangle, the ischioanal fossa lies between the obturator muscle with its medial fascia at the perineal walls, and the levator ani with its inferior fascia.

  • Superomedial boundary: levator ani muscles.
  • Lateral boundary: obturator internus muscle.
  • An anterior recess lies above the perineal membrane.
  • The main part is lateral to levator ani and the external anal sphincter; a posterior part extends above gluteus maximus.
  • The pudendal neurovascular trunk traverses it, with the pudendal canal on its lateral wall.

The perineal membrane is a triangular sheet of dense fibromuscular tissue spanning the anterior half of the pelvic outlet, and it is the reason the posterior vaginal wall and perineal body cannot simply descend.

Step walkthrough

The floor from below upward

Step 1 of 3 · Perineal view

What you see from below

The pelvic diaphragm spans the bony pelvis in two parts: a thin horizontal shelf formed by iliococcygeus, and a thicker U-shaped sling of pubococcygeus and puborectalis around the hiatus.

  • The open area within the U is the levator hiatus, transmitting urethra, vagina and rectum.
  • The part of the hiatus anterior to the perineal body is the urogenital hiatus.
Step 2 of 3 · Deep pouch

Two arch-shaped striated muscles

Just cephalad to the perineal membrane lie two striated muscles that begin posteriorly and pass forward to arch over the urethra.

  • Compressor urethrae and sphincter urethrovaginalis.
  • Both are part of the striated urogenital sphincter and are continuous with the external urethral sphincter; they compress the distal urethra.
  • Posteriorly, skeletal fibres of the transverse vaginal muscle and some smooth muscle intermingle within the membrane itself.
Step 3 of 3 · Attachments

Lateral to bone, medial to viscera

  • Arises from the inner aspect of the inferior ischiopubic rami, superior to the ischiocavernosus muscles and the clitoral crura.
  • Medial attachments: urethra, vaginal walls (roughly at the level of the hymenal ring), and the perineal body.
  • Because of the vagina, it cannot form a continuous sheet closing the anterior pelvis as it does in the man.

By anchoring vagina and perineal body to the bony outlet, it supports the perineal part of the floor against gravity and rises in intra-abdominal pressure.

Pearl

The anterior parts of pubococcygeus and puborectalis lie against the cranial surface of the perineal membrane. Their contraction elevates its medial margin along with the vagina; relaxation allows it to move caudally.

Bedside test

Under anaesthesia, place a finger in the rectum, hook it forward and pull the perineal body gently downward. If the perineal membrane was torn during parturition, an abnormal amount of descent is detectable — the floor sags and the introitus gapes.

The perineal body is the fibromuscular mass bounded by the lower vagina, the perineal skin and the anus — the central point into which many muscles insert.

What attaches to it

Laterally, to bone

Attached to the inferior pubic rami and the ischial tuberosities through the perineal membrane and the superficial transverse perineal muscles.

Anterolaterally

Receives the insertion of the bulbospongiosus muscles.

On its lateral margins

The upper part connects with fibres of the pelvic diaphragm — the puboperinealis portion of pubococcygeus.

Posteriorly

Indirectly attached to the coccyx through the external anal sphincter.

Together these connections anchor the perineal body and its surrounding structures to the bony pelvis and hold it in position.

Naming

The older term central point (tendon) of the perineum is descriptive rather than misleading here — it captures exactly the role of a hub into which multiple muscles insert.

Histology matters

The perineal body encompasses a central fibrous connection between the two halves of the perineal membrane and extends cranially for 2–3 cm above the hymenal ring. Within this segment there is no histological plane of separation between the vaginal wall and the anus.

The pelvic diaphragm has two mechanically different components — a horizontal shelf and a U-shaped sling — and it is the sling that keeps the vagina and rectum closed.

Components of the pelvic diaphragm

The levator plate

  • The region of the levator ani between anus and coccyx, formed by the anococcygeal body and iliococcygeal raphe, is clinically called the levator plate.
  • It forms a supportive shelf on which the rectum, upper vagina and uterus rest.
  • Its relatively horizontal position is set by anterior traction from pubococcygeus and puborectalis, and is important to vaginal and uterine support.
Principle

Normal tone in the pelvic diaphragm holds the base of the U close to the backs of the pubic bones, keeping the vagina and rectum closed. Support failure is as much a loss of that resting tone as it is a failure of any ligament.

Innervation

The levator ani is supplied by an anterior branch of the anterior ramus of the third, fourth and fifth sacral nerves — the nerve to the levator ani — which perforates the muscle from its pelvic surface.

Two sphincters, one striated and one smooth, separated by a palpable groove that is the landmark for repair.

External anal sphincter

A single mass of muscle traditionally divided into three parts.

Subcutaneous part

Attached to perianal skin

Forms an encircling ring around the anal canal and is responsible for the radially oriented folds of perianal skin.

Superficial part

Coccyx to perineal body

Attaches to the coccyx posteriorly, contributing to the anococcygeal body, and sends a few fibres into the perineal body anteriorly.

Deep part

Blends with puborectalis

Its fibres generally encircle the rectum and merge indistinguishably with puborectalis, which loops under the dorsal surface of the anorectum.

At third-degree repair

The superficial part forms the bulk of the anal sphincter as it is seen separated in third-degree midline obstetric tears.

Internal anal sphincter

  • A thickening of the circular smooth muscle of the anal wall, lying just inside the external sphincter.
  • Separated from it by a visible intersphincteric groove.
  • Extends downward inside the external sphincter to within a few millimetres of its caudal extent.
  • The longitudinal smooth muscle of the bowel, with some levator ani fibres, descends between the two sphincters in that groove.
A persistent misnomer

At repair of a chronic fourth-degree laceration the internal sphincter can be identified just outside the anal submucosa as a rubbery white layer — which has often been wrongly called fascia during obstetric repair.

Innervation of the sphincters

The external anal sphincter is supplied by the inferior anal (rectal) nerve, which may be a direct branch of the pudendal or arise independently from the sacral plexus; the same nerve gives cutaneous supply to perianal skin and the anal canal down to the pectinate line. The internal anal sphincter is supplied by the inferior hypogastric plexus.

Almost every clinical consequence in this module traces back to one of three things: a torn membrane, a damaged levator, or a divided sphincter.

Pearl

Support of the pelvic organs depends on the perineal membrane and the perineal body as much as on the levators — which is why both are discussed with the floor rather than with the viscera.

Do not confuse
  • Pelvic diaphragm = levator ani + ischiococcygeus + their fasciae.
  • Perineal membrane = the triangular sheet below it, formerly called the urogenital diaphragm.
  • Levator hiatus = the whole opening within the U; urogenital hiatus = the part anterior to the perineal body.
Why the fibrous system exists at all

A series of visceral ligaments and fasciae tethers the organs and maintains their position over the closed portions of the levators and their covering fasciae. The openings in the pelvic diaphragm and perineal membrane, required for parturition and elimination, necessitated ancillary fibrous elements concentrated over those open areas to support the viscera where the muscular floor cannot. That fibrous system is covered in Module 5.
