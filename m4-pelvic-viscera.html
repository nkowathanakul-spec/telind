Step walkthrough

The vagina and its relations, in thirds

Step 1 of 3 · Orientation

One cavity, interdependent organs

The vagina is a pliable hollow viscus whose shape is determined by the structures around it and by its lateral attachments to the pelvic wall.

  • Because those attachments are to its lateral margins, the lumen is a transverse slit with anterior and posterior walls in contact.
  • The lower vagina is constricted as it passes through the urogenital hiatus; the upper part is much more capacious.
Step 2 of 3 · Axis

A bend, not a straight tube

Anterior traction from the levator ani bends the vagina through about 120° at the junction of the lower third and upper two-thirds.

  • The cervix typically lies within the anterior vaginal wall, making that wall 2–3 cm shorter than the posterior wall.
  • Anterior wall length is about 7–9 cm, with considerable individual variation.
Step 3 of 3 · Relations

Lower, middle and upper thirds

  • Lower third — fused anteriorly with the urethra, posteriorly with the perineal body, laterally to each levator ani by the fibres of Luschka. The part of pubococcygeus attaching to the vagina is the pubovaginalis.
  • Middle third — vesical neck and trigone anteriorly, rectum posteriorly, levators laterally.
  • Upper third — bladder anteriorly, cul-de-sac posteriorly, cardinal ligaments laterally.
Principle

Each viscus can expand largely independently of its neighbours because their attachments to one another are loose. That property is what creates the surgical cleavage planes covered in Module 5.

Vagina, uterus and cervix are best learned as a single continuous tube whose wall changes character along its length.

Pearl

Multiple histological studies over the past hundred years have failed to demonstrate a true fascial layer between bladder and vagina or between vagina and rectum. What the surgeon calls vaginal fascia is the muscularis of the vagina.

Cervical os, canal and the transformation zone

The upper border of the cervical canal is the internal os, above which the narrow canal widens into the endometrial cavity; the lower border is the external os. Squamous epithelium of the vaginal part gives way to columnar epithelium of the canal by squamous metaplasia near the external os. The resulting transformation zone sits variably in relation to the external os and shifts with the hormonal changes of a woman's life.

Where the uterus has no serosa

There is little adventitia in the uterus. The anterior cervix is covered by the bladder and so has no serosa; the broad ligament envelops the lateral aspects of cervix and body, so there is none there either. The posterior cervix does have a serosal covering, because the cul-de-sac peritoneum reflects onto the posterior vaginal wall several centimetres below the cervicovaginal junction.

The broad ligament is a double layer of peritoneum dragged out from the pelvic wall as the müllerian ducts and ovaries migrated to the midline — not a supporting structure.

Mesovarium

Mesentery of the ovary

The ovary is placed dorsally in the consistent arrangement of the three mesenteries.

Mesosalpinx

Mesentery of the tube

The tube lies in the middle and is the most cephalic of the three structures.

Mesoteres

Mesentery of the round ligament

The round ligament is placed ventrally, where it exits the pelvis through the inguinal ligament.

Lateral end

Where the ligament finishes

At the lateral end of tube and ovary the broad ligament ends, where the infundibulopelvic ligament blends with the pelvic wall.

Round ligaments

Extensions of the uterine musculature and the homologue of the gubernaculum testis. They arise as broad bands from each lateral aspect of the anterior corpus, become cylindrical, pass lateral to the deep inferior epigastric vessels into the deep inguinal ring, traverse the canal and enter the subcutaneous tissue of the labium majus. They have little to do with uterine support.

Terminology

Structures at the margins of the broad ligament — including the cardinal and uterosacral ligaments at its lower margin — are visceral ligaments: varying amounts of smooth muscle, vessels, connective tissue and nerves. They are not the pure ligaments of skeletal joints.

The bladder is not layered like a tube but woven, and the functional divide between its dome and its base is a divide in innervation as much as in structure.

Bladder

Wall

A meshwork of intertwining muscle bundles rather than simple layers. The dome is relatively thin when distended; the base is thicker and varies less.

Base

Consists of the trigone and the detrusor loop — a U-shaped band, open posteriorly, forming the bladder base anterior to the intramural ureter. The trigone is smooth muscle arising from the ureters, which occupy two of its three corners.

Vesical neck

Where the urethral lumen traverses the bladder base; the detrusor loop continues as the muscle of the vesical neck and urethra. It rests on the mid vagina.

Shape with filling

Empty, a somewhat flattened disc, slightly concave upward. As it fills, the dome rises off the base and it becomes more spherical.

Principle

Base and dome differ functionally. The base carries α-adrenergic receptors that contract on stimulation and favour continence; the dome responds to β-adrenergic or cholinergic stimulation, contracting to empty the bladder.

Relations

  • Anteriorly — pubic bones and lower abdominal wall.
  • Laterally and inferiorly — pubic bones, abutting obturator internus and levator ani.
  • Posteriorly — vagina and cervix.
  • Apex — joined to the umbilicus by the median umbilical ligament, the urachal remnant.

Urethra

  • Passes through the bladder base for a little under a centimetre as the intramural portion.
  • In its distal two-thirds it is fused with the vagina, sharing derivation from the urogenital sinus.
  • From vesical neck to perineal membrane — which starts at the junction of the middle and distal thirds — the wall has an outer circular skeletal layer (urogenital sphincter) mingling with circular smooth muscle, an inner longitudinal smooth muscle layer, a highly vascular submucosa and oestrogen-responsive nonkeratinised squamous epithelium. The proximal lumen is lined by urothelium.
  • Distal to the perineal membrane the wall is fibrous, forming a nozzle that aims the urinary stream.
Paraurethral glands

Tubular glands in the vascular lamina propria lie on the vaginal surface of the urethra and empty posterolaterally, most prominently over the distal two-thirds. Skene glands are the largest and most distal, draining outside the lumen. Chronic infection can produce urethral diverticula; terminal duct obstruction produces gland cysts, typically lined with stratified squamous epithelium and causing deviation of the urethral opening to the contralateral side.

Ureter — the part within this chapter

A tubular viscus about 25–30 cm long, divided into abdominal and pelvic portions of equal length. The small lumen is surrounded by an inner longitudinal and an outer circular muscle layer. In the abdomen it lies in extraperitoneal connective tissue on the posterior abdominal wall, crossed anteriorly by the left and right colic vessels and the ovarian vessels.

Innervation of bladder and urethra
  • Bladder — the vesical plexus, a component of the inferior hypogastric plexus.
  • Urethral striated muscle — somatic, via the pudendal nerve or direct branches of the sacral plexus.
  • Urethral smooth muscle — the inferior hypogastric plexus.

The colon changes character three times on its way through the pelvis, and each change has a landmark.

Sigmoid colon

Begins its S-shaped curve at the pelvic brim, with three taeniae coli over a circular smooth muscle layer. Unlike much of the colon it has a definite mesentery in its midportion, of variable length and curvature. Supplied by the sigmoid branches of the inferior mesenteric artery.

Rectum

The colon straightens and becomes rectum, extending from the pelvic brim until it loses its anterior peritoneal investment below the cul-de-sac. Two bands of smooth muscle, anterior and posterior; no taeniae coli.

Transverse rectal folds

Three folds containing mucosa, submucosa and circular muscle. The most prominent is the middle one, lying anteriorly on the right about 8 cm above the anus, which must be negotiated at high rectal examination or sigmoidoscopy.

Rectal ampulla

The rectum expands here as it passes posterior to the vagina, beginning under the cul-de-sac peritoneum and filling the posterior pelvis from the side.

Anorectal junction

Bent at about 90°, pulled ventrally by puborectalis fibres attaching to the pubis and posteriorly by the external anal sphincter's dorsal attachment to the coccyx.

Anus

Thickened circular involuntary muscle as the internal anal sphincter; a series of anal valves; at their lower border the pectinate (dentate) line, where colonic mucosa gives way to non-hair-bearing squamous epithelium before becoming hair-bearing perineal skin at the anocutaneous line.

Relations

Rectum and anus lie against the sacrum and levator plate posteriorly and the vagina anteriorly. Each half of the levator ani abuts the lateral wall and sends fibres to mingle with the longitudinal involuntary fibres between internal and external sphincters.

A continuous arterial arcade runs along the lateral border of adnexa, uterus and vagina, linking aortic and internal iliac supply end to end.

Arteries

Ovarian

From the aorta, below the renal arteries

Long retroperitoneal course to the cephalic end of the ovary. Running along the ovarian hilum, it also sends vessels through the mesosalpinx to the tube, including a prominent fimbrial branch.

Ovarian veins

Asymmetric drainage

The accompanying venous plexus drains into the vena cava on the right and into the renal vein on the left.

Uterine

From the internal iliac

Sometimes shares an origin with the internal pudendal or vaginal artery. Joins the uterus near the body–cervix junction, though this position varies with traction. Several large uterine veins accompany it.

Vaginal

Two sources, one arcade

The descending (azygous) branch of the uterine artery along the lateral sulci, plus a vaginal branch of the internal iliac, forming an arcade at the 3 and 9 o’clock positions.

Principle

The uterine artery reaches the lateral border of the uterus after passing over the ureter, giving a small branch to it on the way. Its descending branch then crosses the cervicovaginal junction to lie on the side of the vagina.

Additional vaginal supply

  • Branches merge along the anterior and posterior vaginal walls.
  • The distal vagina also receives internal pudendal supply.
  • The posterior wall receives a contribution from the middle and inferior rectal arteries.

Innervation

The uterus is supplied by the uterovaginal plexus (Frankenhäuser ganglion), which lies in the connective tissue of the cardinal ligament — the anatomical reason pelvic autonomic injury and parametrial dissection are linked.

Bladder arterial supply

From the superior vesical artery, arising from the patent part of the umbilical artery, and the inferior vesical artery, which is either an independent branch of the internal pudendal artery or arises from the vaginal artery. The urethra receives supply both from an inferior extension of the vesical vessels and from the pudendal vessels.
